Processes to assure the safe, effective, and responsible deployment of artificial intelligence (AI) in safety-critical settings are urgently needed. Here we show a procedure to empirically evaluate the impacts of AI augmentation as a basis for responsible deployment. We evaluated three augmentative AI technologies nurses used to recognize imminent patient emergencies, including combinations of AI recommendations and explanations. The evaluation involved 450 nursing students and 12 licensed nurses assessing 10 historical patient cases. With each technology, nurses’ performance was both improved and degraded when the AI algorithm was most correct and misleading, respectively. Our findings caution that AI capabilities alone do not guarantee a safe and effective joint human-AI system. We propose two minimum requirements for evaluating AI in safety-critical settings: (1) empirically measure the performance of people and AI together and (2) examine a range of challenging cases which produce a range of strong, mediocre, and poor AI performance.

Although different AI algorithms may produce different results, similar findings11,23,24,29,43 suggest susceptibility to AI errors might be a common feature of recommendation-centric human-AI architectures43,46,48. All AI algorithms will produce a range of strong, mediocre, and poor performance. This range is likely to include some instances where people perform both better and worse than the AI algorithm alone. Improving the AIalgorithmwilllikelyreducethefrequency ofpoorperformancebutitmay not reduce the range of possible performance or the consequences of poor performance. Therefore, improving the AI algorithm does not necessarily lessen the impactof AI errors. On the contrary, prior research has suggested recognizing and recovering from errors might be more difficult if the frequency of error is rare55,56. Future research should investigate alternative human-AI architectures which might be less susceptible to propagating AI errors48,57,58.

Discussion Despite the potential for complementary human and AI performance, we instead found a combination of helpful and harmful impacts of AI augmentation which corresponded to the difficulty of problem for the AI algorithm. From our testing set, we identified several cases in which nurse traineesaloneperformedbothbetterandworsethantheAIalgorithmalone. From these separate evaluations, nurses and AI appeared to have complementary strengths and weaknesses with the potential for each to compensate for the shortcomings of the other26. However, the three forms of AI augmentation we evaluated did not actualize these potential benefits. We found nurses’ performance was improved by the most correct AI recommendations but degraded by the most misleading AI recommendations. The magnitude of maximum degradationwas nearly twice the magnitudeof maximum improvement. When AI recommendations were most misleading, nurses’ reports of concern for emergency patients were indistinguishable from non-emergency patients, and vice versa. We observed a similar effect of lesser magnitude when nurses were presented with only AI explanations. These results suggest all forms of AI augmentation significantly influenced nurses’ perceptions of patient risk.

Our findings accentuate the risks of using aggregate measures (e.g., overall classification accuracy) to evaluate the impacts of AI augmentation in safety-critical settings. Aggregation applies a judgment about the relative value of disparate effects, often equating benefits and harms in ways that are misaligned with the magnitude of consequences59. For example, we selected cases which varied in difficulty to produce a wide range of AI performance, intentionally over-sampling cases of mediocre or poor AI performance. These cases revealed a combination of AI impacts that both helped and harmed human-AI performance in different circumstances. If we had representatively sampled from the distribution of AI outputs or computed a weighted average of our results based on the probability of occurrence (see Supplementary Fig. 10), the frequent but smaller benefits from correct AI recommendations might have outweighed the rare but larger costs from misleading AI recommendations. However, this aggregation misrepresents how healthcare practitioners are held accountable. Nurses are responsible for patients individually, not in the aggregate. Major harms are not excused by the accumulation of minor benefits. If AI augmentation produces disparate benefits and harms11,23,24,29, aggregate evaluations can obscure or underappreciate the risks of infrequent but severe vulnerabilities.

Our findings suggest significant interactions between nurses and AI interfered with the potential complementarity of the two. With favorable benchmarks for AI performance33,34, explanations38, and interactions12,37, it was reasonable to believe our augmentative AI technology was capable enough to be deployable. However, the strong influence of AI recommendations appeared to propagate AI vulnerabilities and induce dangerous confusions in cases which were otherwise routine for nurses. Our findings reinforce that the interactions between people and augmentative AI technologies can strongly impact performance, undermine apparent redundancies, and even introduce new risks11,23,24,29,43. Due to these interactions, optimizing the performance of AI algorithms alone does not necessarily optimize the performance of the joint human-AI system44,45. Instead, AIbased algorithms and other forms of technology augmentation should be conceived, modeled, designed, and evaluated with people as a joint cognitive system46.ThegoalofdevelopingaugmentativeAItechnologiesshouldnotbe to improve AI algorithm performance, but rather to enable people to effectively accomplish cognitive work.

Collectively, our findings and the methods through which they were empirically revealed suggest minimum evaluation requirements for responsible AI practices. Responsible deployments of augmentative AI technologies in safety-critical settings require decision-makers to be wellinformed about potential risks before people are harmed40. This necessitates conducting pre-deployment evaluations to assess post-deployment risks. The study we conducted is an example of one such evaluation which was able to empirically reveal severe potential risks prior to deployment. Our findings suggest two minimum evaluation requirements for establishing a basis of empirical evidence needed to understand and justify risks of deployment. These requirements were derived from our empirical findings but also have strong theoretical connections to concepts of measurement validity60. Effective and trustworthy evaluations of augmentative AI should at least satisfy these two aspects of well-informed and valid assessments.

First, well-informed evaluations should empirically measure the performance of people and AI together. In safety-critical settings, a growing consensus acknowledges that responsible deployments of AI-based technologies must assist or augment rather than replace people5,10,11,13. However, few evaluations of AI augmentation involve people and even fewer involve domain practitioners15. Though recent trends towards increased practitioner involvement are promising23,28,29,61,62, this has yet to become standard practice in the early evaluation of new AI technologies. Evaluations which measure or compare human and AI performance separately4 must make assumptions about how the resulting joint human-AI system will perform together. Our findings challenge conventional wisdom that improving AI reliability, explainability, or any other capability of a technology alone will necessarily produce a safe and effective joint human-AI system. Instead, our findings caution that even augmentative AI technologies should not be assumedtoalwaysexceedthecapabilitiesofearlypractitionerswitheffective tools, improve performance in all situations, require minimal training investments, facilitate effective human supervision, or support recovery from technology errors. In the absence of reliable methods to predict human-AI performance from the characteristics of each separately, our findings reinforce that understanding the risks of deployment requires empirically evaluating people and AI together as a joint cognitive system46. This is central to establishing construct validity, or the match between a measurement (e.g., pre-deployment evaluation) and target phenomenon (e.g., post-deployment risks)60.

The evaluation procedure we outlined in this study empirically revealed situations in which AI augmentation both improved anddegradedperformance.Ourfindingsillustratethepotentialpositiveand negative impacts of AI augmentation which can be overlooked or underappreciated by evaluations that do not examine the joint capabilities of the human-AI system. This study had several limitations. First, we were only able to recruit a small sample of licensed nurses to supplement our large sample of nursing students. Although we did not find experience to be a strong predictor of human-AI performance, this study was powered to detect only large differences between nursing students and licensed nurses. This study was not instrumented to detect smaller differences between these two groups or differences within experience levels of licensed nurses. Though studies have foundconventionalexperience-basedfactorsdonotreliablypredicthumanAI performance23, future research should continue to investigate the relationships between participant experience, behaviors, interactions with AI algorithms, and other factors which may impact human-AI performance. Second, we did not provide feedbackduringthe study, intentionallylimiting opportunities for learning to best estimate the impacts of AI augmentation. Future research should investigate thedynamicsof how people interact with augmentative AI technologies over time. However, if the benefits of AI augmentation are not immediately actualized or correlated with domain experience23, deployments of augmentative AI technologies in safety-critical settings might ironically increase requirements for training and human expertise65,66. Third, we could only obtain binary ground truth to characterize patient urgency (emergency vs. non-emergency). For patients where a moderately urgent response would be considered best practice, the impacts of AI augmentation in our results could be over-estimated. Fourth, this study presented a computer-based task for remote monitoring of patients which was not situated within the natural clinical environment. We instrumented this study with several aspects of actual clinical work to realistically simulate remote monitoring. This included using actual patient data, real AI predictions and explanations, realistic cognitive work, and variations in difficulty for both nurses and the AI algorithm. However, additional sources of information and environmental context in clinical environments could alter results. Therefore, our results are more likely to generalize to settings where there is physical distance between nurses and patients (e.g., remote monitoring) rather than direct access to patients (e.g., bedside care). Finally, this study was limited to one algorithm, explanation technique, and interface for one setting, target population, and training program. Our choices were representative of common strategies13,33,39, but not exhaustive. Alternative AI algorithms and forms of augmentation could produce different results.Ourfindings reinforce the possibilityof varied and surprising impacts of AI augmentation5,14, which in turn underscores the importance of empirical evaluation.

Second, well-informed evaluations should examine a range of challenging cases which produce a range of strong, mediocre, and poor AI performance. Our findings reaffirm that recognizing and recovering from AI errorsisaconsistentandconsiderablechallenge11,23,24,29,43.Evaluationswhich exclusively consider strong AI performance26, aggregate across disparate regions of AI performance59, or otherwise obscure areas of AI weaknesses are incompleteassessments of risk. All AIalgorithmswillproducea rangeof performance. If practitioners are expected to recognize and recover from AI errors, the impacts must be examined, even if AI errors are expected to be infrequent. The frequency of errors is often underestimated in foresight63. Therefore, evaluating a range of AI performance regardless of frequency is centraltoestablishingcontentvalidity,ortheextenttowhichameasurement samples the entirety of a construct60. If AI augmentation sometimes degrades joint human-AI performance, then organizations seeking to deploy AI technologies face an ethical decision. Although organizations might justify different decisions, failing to consider the range of AI performance is a failure to be well-informed about the potential risks of deployment. In safety-critical settings, underinformed evaluations should not be considered responsible, regardless of AI performance.

If results of an evaluation or other factors lead to retraining or altering the AI algorithm16, the new human-AI system requires additional evaluation. Improving AI performance alone does not necessarily improve the joint performance of people and AI together44,45. While some instances of poor AI performance may uncover opportunitiestoglobally improve the AI algorithm, others may confront hard constraints or trade-offs where improving AI performancein some cases degrades AI performance in other cases. In either case, improving instances of poor AI performance narrows the range of AI performance examined in the evaluation, which violates the second evaluation requirement. Well-informed evaluations must subsequently identify and examine new instances of poor AI performance to broadly cover the new range of possible AI performance. Without examining instances of poor AI performance, evaluations are incomplete assessments of joint human-AI performance26.

Participants and protocol Over 2 years, 450 nursing students and a small sample of 12 licensed nurses were recruited for participation in the study. Figure 3 summarizes the demographics of the participant cohort. Nursing students were enrolled in undergraduate or graduate prelicensure programs progressing towards a Bachelor or Master of Science in Nursing. Participants included second-, third-, and fourth-year undergraduate students and first- and second-year graduate students ranging from 18 to 51 years of age and less than 6 months to over4 yearsof nursingcurriculum completed.Yearsof nursingeducation were estimated by the Assistant Dean of Baccalaureate Nursing Programs based on student course enrollment and prelicensure program. Licensed nurses included Registered Nurses (RN) and Licensed Practical Nurses (LPN) ranging from 33 to 48 years of age and 16 months to 25 years of healthcare work experience. All participants were told that the research was focused on improving the patient data display they were using. Students were required to complete the study as an educational activity for course credit and could voluntarily opt to allow their responses to be used for research by completing an informed consent process. We obtained informed consent from all participants in this study.

We utilized a logistic regression algorithm developed with a humancentered approach to predict imminent patient deterioration31. Training data was obtained from telemetry monitors and the electronic health record ofageneralmedical/surgicalwardofalargemidwesternmedicalcenter.The model was trained to classify patients who would and would not require mobilization of a response team to provide emergency care within five minutes. All data included in the patient data display were available for developing the model. The interpretability of the model, consistency and quality of data in clinical environments, and similarity to clinical practice were considered in choosing the type of model, data, and features, respectively. Logistic regression was selected because of its interpretability47, frequentuseinhealthcareapplications33,69,anddemonstratedsuccessinsimilar safety-critical deployments39,70. Data requirements reflected current data collection practices to maximize the availability of the algorithm and minimize performance discrepancies in clinical settings21. Model features were developed to closely align with how nurses recognize signs of deterioration30,67. The training dataset included a balanced sample of emergency and non-emergency patients with equal weightings for false positive and false negative classifications. Lasso regression71 was used to reduce risks of overfitting and improve model interpretability by removing correlated features and creating a simpler model, respectively. Additional details about the training datasets, modeling assumptions, and humancentered considerations are published elsewhere31,36.

Each of these variables and all possible interactions with variables in the base model were considered as candidate extensions to improve our model. To select among candidate models, we used the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) because the purpose of our modeling was to explain rather than to predict88,89. The final selected model had 4102 degrees of freedom.

To estimate the impacts of AI augmentation on nurses’ reported concern, we used a generalized linear mixed effects model. All possible interactions between AI recommendation (0–100%), patient outcome (emergencyvs.non-emergency), and experimental condition were included as fixed effects. Random intercepts for each participant were included to account for repeated measures and different concern baselines among participants. Another fully crossed random intercept for each case was included to account for any additional aspects of individual cases that might impact concern beyond what was captured by the AI recommendation and patient outcome42,84,85. We normalized concern, centered non-categorical independent variables, and utilized ordered beta regression86 to model nurses’ responses on a continuous closed interval.

A subset of participants (23%) responded on an integer scale rather than a continuous scale, so we used a multiple imputation technique to estimate the impacts of this unintended interval censorship or grouping error. Both the integer and continuous scale appeared identical to participants, but the former permitted participants to record only integer responses. We assumed participants responded with the integer that was closest to the actual continuous concern they would have reported if they had not been censored. We examined collinearities and residuals to evaluate model fitness prior to performing statistical tests. We used the variance inflation factor (VIF) to check for multicollinearities. We found all VIFs among firstorder terms to be less than 5 and all VIFs among interaction terms to be less than 7, suggesting acceptably low correlations among variables (Supplementary Table 2). We also used a simulation-based approach to perform residual diagnostics for hierarchical regression models93,94. Model assumptions were determined not to be violated. We utilized this model to perform contrasts between experimental conditions in the cases which corresponded to the most extreme true positive, false positive, true negative, and false negative AI recommendations. Tests were performed on the log odds ratio scale at the level of individual participant responses (i.e., nurses’ reports of concern for each patient case). Estimates of means and confidence intervals were back-transformed from the logit scale to the response scale.
