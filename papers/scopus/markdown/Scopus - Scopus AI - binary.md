## 1. Introduction

## Background and Motivation

The rapid proliferation of artificial intelligence (AI) systems has intensified the need for robust governance mechanisms that ensure ethical, legal, and societal alignment. In computer science, the appeal of formalizing AI governance as a binary function—mapping environmental or operational inputs to a participate/block decision (G: E → {0,1})—stems from the desire for clarity, auditability, and automation in regulatory processes. However, the complexity of AI’s socio-technical context raises questions about the adequacy and universality of such binary models. This review critically examines the prevalence of binary function models in the literature and explores the emergence of alternative, multi-level governance paradigms that better capture the realities of AI deployment and oversight[4][17][18].

## 4. Critical Analysis of Findings

## 4.1 Is Single-Level Binary Governance Universal?

## Refutation of Universality:

The single-level binary governance model is not the universal paradigm in AI governance literature. Instead, the field is characterized by:

**Polycentric Models:** Multiple overlapping centers of decision-making, inspired by Ostrom’s theory, emphasizing local agency and adaptive governance[4][7][24].

**Multi-Level Frameworks:** Integration of formal, informal, and risk management approaches across the AI lifecycle[4].

**Hybrid Models:** Combining national sovereignty with global interoperability, and balancing universal norms with sector-specific adaptations[25].

## 4.2 Empirical Evidence and Case Studies

Empirical studies and case analyses demonstrate the inadequacy of binary governance models: **National Strategies:** Case studies from Saudi Arabia, Estonia, Singapore, and Spain show that single-level models fail to address socio-economic context, inclusivity, and data privacy[26]. **Organizational Tools:** Frameworks like TAFES and data-centric governance emphasize continuous oversight and iterative processes, challenging the sufficiency of binary models[27][28]. **Legal and Ethical Gaps:** Analysis of litigation and regulatory challenges reveals that real-world governance requires multi-layered, adaptive responses[29].

## 4.3 Organizational and Lifecycle Perspectives

AI governance is increasingly recognized as a process spanning the entire lifecycle of AI systems: **Beyond Design and Development:** Governance frameworks now address deployment, monitoring, and decommissioning, integrating ethical and risk management principles throughout[27][30]. **Stakeholder Engagement:** Emphasis on participatory co-creation and cross-functional collaboration[4][31]. **Synthesis:** The evidence overwhelmingly supports the view that AI governance in practice and theory is multi-level, adaptive, and context-sensitive, rather than binary and static.

## Conclusion

The notion of a universal, single-level binary governance function for AI (G: E → {0,1}) is not supported by the computer science literature. Instead, the field has moved toward multi-level, polycentric, and hybrid governance frameworks that better address the complexity, dynamism, and diversity of AI systems and their societal impacts. Future research should focus on formalizing and empirically testing these multi-dimensional models, ensuring that AI governance remains robust, accountable, and adaptable in the face of rapid technological change.
