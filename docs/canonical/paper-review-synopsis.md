The 18 systems from Chapter 2 Table 2.1, alphabetical by first author. | Abella et al. | Bajcsy & Fisac (2024) [[notes]](../../notes/Human%E2%80%93AI%20Safety-%20A%20Descendant%20of%20Generative%20AI%20and%20Control%20Systems%20Safety.md) | Defines the Human-AI Safety Filter as a control-theoretic mechanism that monitors AI outputs and overrides them at the last possible moment to prevent trajectory-level safety violations. | Sophisticated Level-1 gate — the ceiling of what participation control can achieve without touching advisory scope. The alternation is triggered by syntactic delimiter tokens rather than classified environmental state, so the conditioning variable differs. Proposition 3.1 is separately valuable as a computational-theoretic argument for why binary output constraint is suboptimal. | Nearest formal precursor to the containment structure of A_AI, and the only paper in the corpus offering a complexity-theoretic proof that binary output constraint degrades AI capability. |

The nested-permission containment is a direct analogue of A_AI(SAFE) ⊃ A_AI(CAUTION) ⊃ A_AI(UNSAFE). The critical difference is the conditioning variable: Baxi conditions on offline-verified agent robustness, we condition on classified environmental state. Baxi answers "how much can this AI be trusted to do?"; we answer "what is the AI permitted to do given current environmental conditions?" These are orthogonal questions. | Would be the strongest counter-example to our novelty claim without the conditioning-variable distinction; including it is essential to preclude the "isn't this just CGAE?" objection. |
| Chen, Kang & Li (2025) — SHIELDAGENT [[notes]](../../notes/SHIELDAGENT-%20Shielding%20Agents%20via%20Verifiable%20Safety%20Policy%20Reasoning.md) | Extracts LTL rules from policy documents and uses formal verification to evaluate each planned agent action against the derived rule circuits, producing a binary safe / unsafe label per action. | Policy-driven per-action validation, sophisticated in its formal-methods stack. The governance operates at the individual-action level with no restriction of the agent's action type space: the shield fires per action, and the recommendation space is not restricted before reasoning. | State-of-the-art in LTL-based policy shielding; illustrates the pattern of per-action validation that does not condition on environmental state. |
| Corsi et al. (2024) — Verification-Guided Shielding [[notes]](../../notes/Verification-Guided%20Shielding%20for%20Deep%20Reinforcement%20Learning.md) | Refines the shielding paradigm by using formal DNN verification to partition the input space into provably safe and potentially unsafe regions, activating the shield only in the verified-unsafe regions and reducing overhead by 25–71%. | Shield refinement improves efficiency but retains binary character: the shield either intervenes or permits, with no intermediate response. The verified-safe / potentially-unsafe partition is a preprocessing step, not a runtime governance mode. | Demonstrates that even sophisticated shield refinements do not transcend binary governance; anticipates the "but modern shields are more nuanced" objection. |
| Dalrymple et al. (2024) — Guaranteed Safe AI [[notes]](../../notes/Towards%20Guaranteed%20Safe%20AI-%20A%20Framework%20for%20Ensuring%20Robust%20and%20Reliable%20AI%20Systems.md) | Proposes the Guaranteed Safe AI framework built on three components: a world model, a safety specification, and a verifier that produces auditable proof certificates before AI output is deployed. | GS AI is the theoretical umbrella our architecture instantiates. The framework itself is binary at the verification level: proof certificates either verify or fail. Our architecture is a state-conditioned instantiation of GS principles that fills a gap the framework leaves open — how to govern under conditions that are elevated but not prohibitive. | The closest theoretical precedent; positioning our architecture as a domain-specific instantiation of GS principles is a core framing decision. |
| Feng, McDonald & Zhang (2025) — Levels of Autonomy [[notes]](../../notes/Levels%20of%20Autonomy%20for%20AI%20Agents.md) | Proposes a five-level autonomy framework for AI agents with two governance dimensions (agency and autonomy) and introduces "autonomy certificates" as formal credentials for agent capabilities. | The most conceptually granular authority-allocation framework in the corpus. Both dimensions are configured at design time and do not vary with operational conditions — a Level-3 agent stays Level-3 regardless of whether the environment is safe or dangerous. Their two-dimension decomposition makes it easy to state precisely what our contribution adds: advisory-scope restriction is a missing third governance lever. | Most granular authority-allocation framework identified; its explicit two-dimension decomposition makes the missing-third-lever argument concrete rather than abstract. |
| Flehmig et al. (2024) [[notes]](../../notes/Implementing%20Artificial%20Intelligence%20in%20Safety-Critical%20Systems%20during%20Operation-%20Challenges%20and%20Extended%20Framework%20for%20a%20Quality%20Assurance%20Process.md) | Implements a traffic-light degradation index for AI in safety-critical systems, classifying AI performance into three levels (green / orange / red) using non-AI statistical methods; at orange, the supervisory component conducts thorough checks; at red, the system switches to a non-AI backup. | The closest structural precedent — three-level classification triggered by monitoring, with an intermediate level. The intermediate level governs supervisory intensity (how closely the human watches), not advisory scope (what the AI may recommend). The AI's output is identical at green and orange. Central to our F-15 empirical finding: a Flehmig-style baseline is output-equivalent to a plain binary gate (0.00% divergence over 5 years of hourly data), which converts the CAUTION-mode gap from a conceptual claim into an empirical measurement. | The structurally closest precedent in the corpus; makes the "why is your CAUTION mode different from Flehmig's orange?" question inescapable, and gives our empirical F-15 result its comparison target. |
| Gyllenhammar et al. (2025) [[notes]](../../notes/The%20Road%20to%20Safe%20Automated%20Driving%20Systems-%20A%20Review%20of%20Methods%20Providing%20Safety%20Evidence.md) | Reviews safety-evidence methods for automated driving, identifying a three-tier hierarchy of Operational Design Domain (ODD), Restricted Operational Domain (ROD), and Minimal Risk Condition (MRC). | Three-level degradation hierarchy — closest to graduated governance in the automated-driving literature — but the intermediate ROD restricts *where* the vehicle operates (geographic domain confinement), not *what* recommendations the system produces within its operational scope. Operational-domain confinement is a different governance object from advisory-scope restriction. | Comparator for domain-confinement adaptation; shows that "three levels" in adjacent literature typically restricts operational scope rather than advisory type space. |
| Hamel-De le Court, Belardinelli & Goodall (2025) [[notes]](../../notes/Probabilistic%20Shielding%20for%20Safe%20Reinforcement%20Learning.md) | Extends the shielding paradigm to stochastic environments, constructing a probabilistic shield with two formal guarantees — Theorem 1 (shielded policy satisfies safety constraints with probability ≥ 1 − δ) and Theorem 2 (convergence of the underlying learning algorithm is preserved). | The most formally rigorous shield extension to stochastic settings, yet the intervention mechanism remains binary: the shield either intervenes when estimated risk exceeds a threshold or permits the action. Probabilistic formulation of the safety guarantee does not change the binary character of the governance decision. | Rules out the "but modern shields handle stochasticity, which gives graduated behaviour" argument; the guarantee is probabilistic but the decision is not. |
| Könighofer et al. (2025) [[notes]](../../notes/Shields%20for%20Safe%20Reinforcement%20Learning.md) | Formalises shields as runtime mechanisms synthesised from LTL safety specifications and environment models that intercept unsafe actions before they reach the environment. | The canonical reference for the shield formalism. Every shield-derived governance mechanism in the corpus builds on this. The mechanism is binary by construction: the shield either allows the action or replaces it with a safe alternative; no intermediate mode is definable within the framework. | The reference implementation of Level-1 binary governance; any comparison to shielding starts here. |
| Kwon et al. (2025) [[notes]](../../notes/Runtime%20Safety%20through%20Adaptive%20Shielding-%20From%20Hidden%20Parameter%20Inference%20to%20Provable%20Guarantees.md) | Adaptive shielding framework that incorporates environment-centric features E ∈ ℝⁿ² into a SafetyScore driving runtime shield adjustment via an Adaptive Constraint Parameter (ACP) threshold Γ_t; Theorem 1 provides a formal safety guarantee that the shielded policy satisfies constraints throughout the adaptation. | The closest near-miss in the corpus: environment-centric features as governance input, runtime adaptation, and a formal safety proof — all three properties we claim. Two critical differences remain. First, the SafetyScore is binary (each state-action pair is classified safe or unsafe, with no intermediate). Second, the adaptation is quantitative (ACP threshold Γ_t shifts along a continuous scale) rather than qualitative (no discrete governance modes analogous to SAFE, CAUTION, UNSAFE). Kwon adapts *how strictly* the shield intervenes; we adapt *what the AI is permitted to recommend*. | Would be the strongest technical counter-example ("this paper already uses environment as governance input") without our binary-vs-graduated and quantitative-vs-qualitative distinctions; retained to preclude that objection. |
| Odriozola-Olalde et al. Confirms that "graduated" in shielded RL means implementation-tier gradation, not advisory-scope gradation. | Anchor-adjacent SLR that pre-empts the reading "surely shielded RL literature already has three-level governance" — it does not, in the sense we mean. |
| Perez-Cerrolaza et al. (2024) [[notes]](../../notes/Artificial%20Intelligence%20for%20Safety-Critical%20Systems%20in%20Industrial%20and%20Transportation%20Domains-%20A%20Survey.md) | Survey of AI safety techniques across industrial and transportation domains (294 references), classifying AI safety compliance into three classes and cataloguing safety bags, monitors, and envelopes as dominant runtime mechanisms. | Anchor SLR. The scale of the review (294 references) is what makes it evidentially strong — any counter-example would have to hide in a corpus this large without leaving a trace. | One of the three anchor systematic reviews that make the absence claim credible; without it, our claim would be a survey of primary comparators rather than a structural finding across the literature. |
| Ramos et al. (2024) [[notes]](../../notes/Collaborative%20Intelligence%20for%20Safety-Critical%20Industries-%20A%20Literature%20Review.md) | Systematic review of 91 studies on collaborative intelligence in safety-critical industries, identifying two primary interaction modes (machine-assists-human and human-assists-machine) across six industries. | Anchor SLR. | Shamsujjoha et al. (2025) — Swiss Cheese Model [[notes]](../../notes/Swiss%20Cheese%20Model%20for%20AI%20Safety-%20A%20Taxonomy%20and%20Reference%20Architecture%20for%20Multi-Layered%20Guardrails%20of%20Foundation%20Model%20Based%20Agents.md) | Multi-layered guardrail taxonomy for foundation-model agents, cataloguing 13 distinct guardrail actions across 14 pipeline target categories. | The definitive Level-2 (output-restriction) taxonomy — the most comprehensive catalogue of what modern guardrails can do. | Wang et al. (2026) — AgentSpec [[notes]](../../notes/AgentSpec-%20Customizable%20Runtime%20Enforcement%20for%20Safe%20and%20Reliable%20LLM%20Agents.md) | Domain-specific language for runtime enforcement over LLM agents, allowing developers to define structured rules composed of triggers, predicates, and enforcement actions; prevents over 90% of unsafe code executions in the reported evaluation. | State-of-the-art in per-action runtime enforcement. ## Reading rules

- **Ordering.** Both tables are alphabetical by first author, matching Table 2 of `review-protocol.md` and Table 3a of `master-coding-table.md`.
- **Notes link.** Each row's `[[notes]]` link opens the paper's full extraction notes. Follow it for the paper's raw contribution details, methodology, per-dimension governance analysis, and citation trail.
- **Framing convention.** *What the paper does* is written in our framing so that the description maps directly onto our comparison scheme. Papers themselves may use different vocabulary; we prefer to translate consistently.
- **What "Our comment" covers.** Interpretation, not description. What is right about the approach, what is missing relative to our architecture, and — where relevant — why the missing part is load-bearing for our contribution. Comments should be defensible against the paper's own claims; the extraction notes carry the evidence.
- **What "Why we selected it" covers.** The argumentative role the paper plays in the review, in one sentence. Not the paper's importance in general; the paper's importance *to our argument*.
